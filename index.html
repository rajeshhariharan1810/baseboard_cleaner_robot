Design intent

The device is built around a single rotating brush that sweeps debris out of the seam between a wall and its baseboard trim, guided along the wall by two pairs of free-spinning side wheels. The front face is drafted 15° outward so the body glides past inside corners instead of catching on them. Everything downstream — the drive wheel spacing, the brush slot, the interior layout — follows from that basic idea rather than being decided independently.

Problem-solving along the way

A handful of the more instructive decisions in this build, kept because the reasoning behind each one is more useful than a smooth final render on its own.

01

Axle bracket cradle built on the wrong sketch plane

Problem
The U-shaped brackets that cradle the drive axle were sketched on a plane that didn't match the axle's actual rotation axis, misaligning the cradle geometry entirely.
Reasoning
The axle runs along the chassis's width axis, so its cross-section needed a sketch plane perpendicular to that axis — not parallel to it, which is what the original plane gave.
Decision
Rebuilt both bracket sketches on the corrected plane and verified alignment from a front-view screenshot before committing to the extrude.
Tradeoff
None — caught before any other geometry depended on it.
02

Motor and brush shaft competing for the same interior space

Problem
A direct in-line coupling between the motor and the brush shaft needed more axial length than the chassis interior actually had — the brush and shaft alone nearly filled the available width.
Reasoning
Checked the actual numbers rather than the layout as drawn: roughly 77mm of usable interior width against 90mm-plus needed for brush, shaft, and motor placed end to end.
Decision
Moved the motor onto a perpendicular axis near one end of the brush instead of forcing a straight-through fit — closer to how compact devices actually route power through an offset drive.
Tradeoff
The physical coupling between motor and brush shaft is left as a positioned approximation rather than a modeled mechanism.
03

Battery cell height exceeding the shell height

Problem
An upright 18650 cell is 65mm tall; the body shell it needed to fit inside is only 55mm.
Reasoning
Checked the real dimensions of the component being placed against a shell height that was already fixed, rather than assuming the two would agree.
Decision
Reoriented both cells to lie on their side — which is also closer to how most compact devices actually package cylindrical cells.
Tradeoff
Required reworking the battery tray's cavity orientation to match.
04

A mounting hole larger than the bracket meant to hold it

Problem
The motor mounting bracket's crossbar was specified at 8mm tall, but needed to contain a 22.2mm clearance hole for the motor body.
Reasoning
Worked backward from the wall thickness the hole actually needed around it, instead of treating the original bracket dimensions as fixed.
Decision
Redesigned the bracket as a single plate with adequate material around the bore, rather than patching the original two-piece shape.
Tradeoff
Departs from the original reference sketch, but is structurally sound.

What's deliberately simplified

A model built for a portfolio, not for manufacturing — these are the places that show, stated plainly rather than left for someone else to find.

  • cosmeticThe helical bristle ridge on the brush core is a surface detail for the render, not a modeled bristle field.
  • non-functionalThe lid's snap clips are shown at their final seated geometry — flex isn't simulated.
  • approximatedThe motor-to-brush drive is shown as a positioned gap between two shaft ends, not a modeled coupling.
  • duplicatedThe two battery cells are identical repeated instances, not independently modeled or wired.
